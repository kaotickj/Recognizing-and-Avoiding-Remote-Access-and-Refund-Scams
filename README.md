# Protecting Yourself from Refund and Remote Access Scams

> An overview of refund scams, remote access scams, the social engineering techniques used by scammers, how these scams have evolved, and what you can do to recognize, prevent, and report them.

## Preface

I have spent more than three decades working with computers and cybersecurity. During that time, I have watched online fraud evolve along with the technology used to commit it.

This bulletin focuses on two related but distinct types of scams: **refund scams** and **remote access scams**.

They often use the same social engineering techniques, and in some cases a refund scam may involve remote access to the victim's computer. That does not make them the same scam. A refund scam is based on convincing the victim that money was accidentally refunded or credited to them and that they must return the difference. A remote access scam is based on convincing the victim to grant someone remote control of their computer or device.

Both depend heavily on social engineering.

The scammer's objective is to manipulate the victim into doing something they would not normally do, whether that means sending money, providing sensitive information, installing software, or granting access to a computer.

My interest in these scams comes from research and investigation into how they work and how their techniques have changed over time. Understanding the methods used by scammers is useful because it allows us to recognize the same patterns when they appear in a different form.

## The History of Social Engineering and Online Scams

Social engineering has existed for much longer than the internet.

What changed with the internet was the scale and the number of ways scammers could reach potential victims.

### The Early Internet

In the late 1980s, the internet was still in its infancy. I had just begun learning computer programming, and online communication was very different from what we have today.

Bulletin Board Systems, or BBSes, provided some of the earliest online communities. People could communicate, exchange files, and share information with others who had access to the same systems.

As more people went online, scammers had a larger population to target.

The early 1990s brought another major change with the development and widespread use of HTML. Creating websites became much more accessible, and people could publish information without needing specialized knowledge of older web technologies.

That was a major benefit to the internet, but the same accessibility also gave scammers new opportunities.

They could create websites and online communications that looked legitimate and use them to deceive people.

In 1994, Yahoo entered the scene as a web directory. Originally called "Jerry and David's Guide to the World Wide Web," it eventually developed into a larger web portal offering multiple services.

The internet was rapidly becoming something ordinary households could access.

People were gathering in communities, websites, forums, and chat rooms.

That environment also provided scammers with a growing population of people who were still learning how to recognize online fraud.

### The Evolution of Social Engineering

Early social engineering techniques included pretexting, phishing, and baiting.

As security technology improved, scammers adapted.

Modern attacks may involve highly targeted phishing, spear-phishing, vishing, social media impersonation, and other forms of deception.

The technology changes, but the basic objective remains the same:

**Get the victim to trust the scammer long enough to do what the scammer wants.**

# Refund Scams

Refund scams are based on a simple deception.

The victim is convinced that a legitimate transaction has occurred and that the victim is now entitled to, or has already received, a refund.

The scammer then creates a problem with that supposed refund.

A common scenario is:

> "We accidentally refunded you too much."

The scammer then claims that the victim must return the difference.

The amount may be presented as a mistake made by the company, the bank, or the victim.

The scammer's objective is to convince the victim that the money exists and that the victim is responsible for correcting the error.

It does not.

The victim is being manipulated into sending the scammer money.

The scammer may use fear, urgency, or threats to make the victim comply. The victim may be told that failure to return the money will result in legal problems, account closure, or some other consequence.

The specific story can change.

The underlying technique does not.

### How Refund Scams Begin

A refund scam may begin with an email that appears to come from a legitimate company.

Common examples in these scams include brands such as:

* Geek Squad
* Norton
* Amazon
* McAfee

The email may contain the company's logo and other information intended to make it appear legitimate.

The message may claim that a purchase or subscription has been renewed and provide instructions for canceling it or requesting a refund.

A phone number is often included.

That phone number connects the victim to the scammer.

### The Fake Overpayment

Once the victim is on the phone, the scammer establishes the scenario.

The victim may be told that a refund has been processed.

The scammer then creates the supposed mistake.

The victim is told that too much money was refunded.

The scammer now has a reason to demand money from the victim.

This is the central deception.

The victim is not returning money that was actually over-refunded.

The victim is sending money to the scammer because the scammer convinced them that an overpayment occurred.

### Manipulating What the Victim Sees

In some refund scams, the scammer may use remote access or browser developer tools as part of the deception.

For example, while the victim is viewing their bank's website, the scammer can manipulate the information displayed in the browser.

The victim may then see a refund amount that is much larger than expected.

The scammer can claim that they accidentally entered the wrong amount.

The victim is then made to believe that they caused the problem and must correct it.

This is a form of sleight of hand.

The money shown on the screen is not proof that the bank actually deposited that amount.

The scammer has manipulated what the victim sees.

### The Financial Demand

Once the victim believes there has been an overpayment, the scammer tells them how to return the difference.

The victim may be instructed to:

* Make a wire transfer
* Purchase gift cards
* Withdraw money from a bank
* Send money to another person

The recipient may be a money mule acting on behalf of the scammer.

The scammer's objective is to turn the fabricated refund into an actual financial loss for the victim.

# Remote Access Scams

Remote access scams are different.

The defining feature is the attempt to obtain remote control of the victim's computer or device.

The scammer may claim to be:

* Technical support
* Customer service
* A financial institution
* Another trusted organization

The scammer creates a reason why the victim supposedly needs assistance.

The victim is then instructed to install remote-access software or otherwise provide remote access.

Once access has been granted, the scammer may be able to interact with the computer, access information, manipulate transactions, or install additional software.

The important point is that applications such as AnyDesk and TeamViewer are legitimate remote-access tools.

The problem is not the software itself.

The problem is giving an unknown person remote access to your computer.

---

# Scammers and Remote Access Tools

Scammers frequently use legitimate remote-support applications as part of their attacks.

## AnyDesk

A scammer may tell the victim to download and install AnyDesk.

The victim may then be instructed to provide the AnyDesk ID and password generated by the application.

The scammer uses those credentials to connect to the victim's computer.

The victim may believe they are giving access to a legitimate technician.

They are actually giving access to the scammer.

## TeamViewer

TeamViewer can be abused in much the same way.

The scammer convinces the victim to install the software and then asks for the TeamViewer ID and password.

Once connected, the scammer can interact with the victim's computer.

## The Important Distinction

AnyDesk, TeamViewer, and similar applications are not inherently malicious.

They are legitimate tools.

The warning sign is the situation in which they are being used.

If you did not contact the company first and someone unexpectedly tells you to install remote-access software, that should be treated as a serious warning sign.

Legitimate technical support should not require you to surrender control of your computer to an unsolicited caller simply because that caller says they are technical support.

# Common Scam Techniques

Refund scams and remote access scams can use many of the same social engineering techniques.

## Emotional Manipulation

Scammers use emotions to influence the victim's decisions.

They may create sympathy, fear, embarrassment, or a sense of personal connection.

The objective is to make the victim less likely to question the story.

## Urgency and Fear

Scammers frequently create an artificial deadline.

The victim may be told that:

* An account will be closed
* Legal action will be taken
* Money will be lost
* A computer is infected
* A problem must be fixed immediately

The purpose is to prevent the victim from stopping to verify the claim.

## Impersonation

Scammers may impersonate:

* Government agencies
* Banks
* Technology companies
* Retailers
* Antivirus companies
* Customer-service departments

They may use company names, logos, employee names, phone numbers, or other information to appear legitimate.

## Fake Offers and Rewards

Scammers may claim that the victim has won a prize, is entitled to a refund, or has received some other unexpected benefit.

The offer is used to get the victim to engage with the scammer.

## Tech Support Scams

The scammer claims that the victim's computer has a problem.

The supposed solution requires the victim to allow the scammer remote access.

The actual objective is unauthorized access to the victim's computer.

## Phishing

Phishing uses fraudulent emails, messages, websites, or pop-ups to imitate legitimate organizations.

The victim may be directed to click a link, open an attachment, or provide credentials.

# Scam Lifecycle

Although individual scams vary, many follow a recognizable sequence.

## 1. Initial Contact

The scammer contacts the victim through a phone call, email, text message, or pop-up.

The contact is often unsolicited.

## 2. Establishing Trust

The scammer presents a believable identity or scenario.

They may claim to represent a legitimate company and provide information intended to make the story appear authentic.

## 3. Creating Urgency

The scammer introduces a problem that supposedly requires immediate action.

This prevents the victim from taking time to verify the situation.

## 4. Obtaining Cooperation

This is where the scams diverge.

In a **refund scam**, the victim is convinced that a refund or overpayment exists and that the victim must correct the situation.

In a **remote access scam**, the victim is convinced to install remote-access software or otherwise provide access to the computer.

A scam can also combine these techniques. For example, a refund scam may use remote access to manipulate what the victim sees on their computer.

## 5. Exploitation

The scammer uses the situation they have created to achieve the actual objective.

That may involve stealing information, accessing accounts, manipulating transactions, or convincing the victim to send money.

## 6. Executing the Scam

The victim carries out the requested action.

That may be providing information, transferring money, purchasing gift cards, or giving the scammer access to the computer.

## 7. Victim Realization

The victim may eventually notice unauthorized activity, realize that the supposed refund does not exist, or otherwise recognize that they have been deceived.

## 8. Damage Control and Reporting

The victim should contact financial institutions, secure affected accounts, preserve evidence, and report the incident.

# Red Flags and Warning Signs

You don't need to identify every possible scam.

Learn to recognize the warning signs.

## 1. Unsolicited Communications

An unexpected email, phone call, or pop-up should immediately make you cautious.

If you did not initiate the conversation, don't assume the person contacting you is legitimate.

## 2. High-Pressure Tactics

If someone is pressuring you to act immediately, stop.

The urgency may be part of the scam.

## 3. Requests for Personal Information

Be cautious when someone unexpectedly asks for:

* Social Security numbers
* Passwords
* Banking information
* Other financial information

## 4. Offers That Are Too Good to Be True

Unexpected prizes, refunds, or other financial benefits should be treated with skepticism.

If you never entered a contest, for example, you probably did not suddenly win one.

## 5. Unusual Payment Requests

Requests for gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers, or other unusual payment methods are warning signs.

## 6. Poor Spelling and Grammar

Poor spelling and grammar can sometimes indicate that a message is fraudulent.

It should not be treated as proof by itself, but it is one additional warning sign.

## 7. Unexpected Attachments or Links

Don't open unexpected attachments or click suspicious links.

They may lead to phishing sites or contain malware.

## 8. Caller ID Spoofing

Caller ID does not prove who is calling.

A scammer can make a call appear to come from a legitimate company or organization.

## 9. Impersonation of Trusted Brands

A message saying "Microsoft Support" or "Amazon" does not mean Microsoft or Amazon actually contacted you.

Verify the contact independently.

## 10. Fear-Based Tactics

A pop-up telling you that your computer is infected and that you must call a number immediately is a classic example.

Don't panic.

Don't call the number displayed in the warning.

Take a step back and verify the situation independently.

# Preventing Scams

## 1. Be Skeptical

Treat unsolicited communications with caution, particularly when they involve urgency, money, or remote access.

Take time to evaluate the situation.

## 2. Verify Identities Independently

If someone claims to represent a company, find the company's official contact information yourself.

Do not rely on the contact information supplied by the caller or email.

## 3. Protect Personal Information

Don't provide sensitive information unless you have independently verified who you are dealing with and why they need it.

## 4. Use Strong Passwords

Use strong, unique passwords for online accounts.

A reputable password manager can help.

Enable two-factor authentication when available.

## 5. Keep Software Updated

Keep your operating system, applications, and security software updated.

## 6. Educate Yourself

Scam techniques change.

Stay informed about current scams and common social engineering techniques.

## 7. Use Trusted Security Software

Use reputable antivirus and anti-malware software.

## 8. Secure Your Wi-Fi

Use a strong password to protect your wireless network.

## 9. Don't Share Remote Access Credentials

Never give an unsolicited caller your AnyDesk, TeamViewer, or other remote-access credentials.

If you need technical support, initiate the support request yourself and verify who you are dealing with.

## 10. Report Suspicious Activity

Report scams to the appropriate authorities and organizations.

## 11. Trust Your Instincts

If something doesn't make sense, stop.

You are allowed to hang up.

You are allowed to take time to verify the information.

You are allowed to say no.

# Recognizing Fraudulent Call Centers

Some remote-access and refund scams operate through organized call centers.

These operations may consist of multiple groups or "cells" that handle different parts of the scam.

One group may handle initial contact.

Another may build trust with the victim.

Another may handle the actual financial portion of the scam.

These operations can operate across multiple countries.

Some known scam operations have been associated with locations such as New Delhi, India.

## Characteristics of Potentially Fraudulent Call Centers

### Unsolicited Calls

The call begins without the victim having requested assistance.

### Scripted Conversations

The caller may follow a predetermined script.

### High-Pressure Tactics

The caller attempts to force the victim into making an immediate decision.

### Lack of Transparency

The caller may avoid providing verifiable information about who they are or what organization they represent.

### Requests for Remote Access

The caller asks the victim to install remote-access software or provide access credentials.

### Unauthorized Transactions

The caller attempts to direct the victim toward financial transactions.

### Insistence on Confidentiality

The caller tells the victim not to discuss the situation with anyone else.

That can prevent friends, family members, banks, or legitimate technical-support personnel from recognizing the scam.

# Geolocation Awareness

Geolocation can sometimes provide useful information when investigating suspicious activity, but it has limitations.

## IP Addresses

Devices connected to the internet use IP addresses.

An IP address can sometimes be used to determine an approximate geographic location.

Geolocation databases associate IP address ranges with approximate geographic regions.

This information may provide a country, city, or region associated with an IP address.

## Understanding the Information

If the geographic information associated with an IP address does not match the location claimed by someone contacting you, that may be useful information.

However, it does not prove that the person is a scammer.

IP geolocation is approximate.

VPNs and other technologies can also obscure the actual location of the person using an IP address.

Geolocation should therefore be treated as one piece of information rather than definitive proof of someone's physical location.

# Reporting Scams

Reporting scams is important because it gives authorities information about fraudulent activity and may help identify larger operations.

## Local Law Enforcement

If you have experienced financial loss, identity theft, or another crime, contact your local law-enforcement agency.

Provide as much information as possible.

Preserve emails, phone numbers, screenshots, transaction records, and other evidence.

## Federal Trade Commission

In the United States, scams can be reported to the Federal Trade Commission.

[Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The FTC collects information about fraud and shares information with law-enforcement agencies.

## Internet Crime Complaint Center

The Internet Crime Complaint Center, or IC3, accepts complaints involving internet-related crimes.

[Internet Crime Complaint Center](https://www.ic3.gov?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

## Social Media and Websites

If a scam involves a social-media account, advertisement, website, or other online service, report the activity through that platform's reporting system.

## Consumer Protection Agencies

Depending on where you live, there may also be a local or regional consumer-protection agency that accepts reports involving scams and fraud.

# Conclusion

Refund scams and remote-access scams are different scams, but they often rely on the same basic weakness: human trust.

A refund scam convinces you that a financial transaction has occurred and that you must correct an alleged error.

A remote-access scam convinces you to give someone control of your computer or device.

Both can use impersonation, urgency, fear, and other forms of social engineering.

The most important thing you can do is slow down.

If someone unexpectedly contacts you about a refund, don't assume the refund exists.

If someone unexpectedly contacts you about a computer problem, don't give them remote access.

If someone tells you that you made a mistake and now owe them money, verify the transaction independently.

If someone tells you that you must act immediately, stop and verify the claim.

Don't rely on caller ID.

Don't rely on an email logo.

Don't rely on a phone number supplied by the caller.

Don't give an unsolicited caller your passwords, financial information, or remote-access credentials.

And if you realize that you have been scammed, don't let embarrassment prevent you from taking action.

Contact your financial institution if money is involved. Secure your accounts. Preserve the evidence. Report the incident.

The technology will continue to change.

The scams will continue to change.

The basic principles remain the same:

**Stop. Think. Verify.**

You don't have to trust someone simply because they sound professional.

You don't have to follow instructions simply because someone claims to be an authority.

And you don't have to solve a problem that a stranger just told you that you have.
